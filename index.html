2026 NASA Space Apps Challenge · Flame in Freefall: SI-Powered Fire Safety Insights from Microgravity Combustion Data

Decades of NASA fire experiments in space, turned into answers a habitat designer can use.

Fire behaves differently without gravity: no rising hot gas, no flickering teardrop, and a flame that lives or dies with the cabin ventilation. NASA has measured this in orbit for years, and the results sit in the Physical Sciences Informatics (PSI) repository. FLARE-SI summarises, ranks and interprets those investigations, and lets you test what they imply in a simulator built on the same data.

Three findings to start with

In microgravity, ventilation is the fire's oxygen supply

BASS-II flames on plastic rods and slabs went out when airflow dropped to roughly 0.2–0.6 cm/s, even at about 20 % oxygen. On Earth, buoyancy would keep feeding them.

Evidence: BASS-II (PSI-25), 13 low-flow extinction tests on rods and slabs

Earth tests can mislead in both directions

SAFFIRE-II silicone sheets burned in normal gravity but did not spread in microgravity. Other materials burn in microgravity at airflow speeds too low to matter on Earth.

Evidence: SAFFIRE-II (PSI-99), samples 2-1 to 2-4

The Moon-base atmosphere is barely covered

A base at 30 % oxygen or more is planned. In the PSI tables, solid-fuel tests stop at 22.2 % oxygen. Only 17 FLEX droplet tests (none of which self-extinguished) and the ACME gas flames reach those conditions, and nothing was measured in partial gravity.

What the data says: interpreted findings

The data behind FLARE-SI

Everything on this site comes from the experimental tables of NASA's Physical Sciences Informatics repository, combustion science area. This page says what we took, how, and what state it is in.

Source

NASA PSI repository, managed by NASA Glenn Research Center. Each investigation has an accession number (for example PSI-25), a DOI, an experimental table (CSV) and folders of raw data, documents and publications.

Licence

All investigations used here are published under Creative Commons Zero (CC0-1.0), as stated in each investigation's metadata. Tables were retrieved on 14 November 2026.

What we used

The experimental-table CSV of each investigation, plus its descriptive metadata (title, objective, hardware, publication count). We did not process the raw videos or sensor files, which total several terabytes.

Tables used

How the tables were prepared

Solid-fuel outcomes (BASS-II, SAFFIRE)

  • The BASS-II table logs airflow as free text ("5 down to ext", "blowoff fan pot 6.0"). We kept only the 38 tests where the log states an unambiguous outcome and added 3 SAFFIRE tests, giving 41 curated outcomes.
  • Where only a fan setting was logged, flow was estimated as u ≈ 3.9 × (pot − 0.2) cm/s, calibrated on rows that list both (O5a, P5, O20).
  • The initial calibrated O₂ value is used for each test.
  • Each curated row keeps its test ID so it can be checked against the PSI table.

Droplet tests (FLEX)

  • All 274 rows are included as published: fuel, pressure, O₂ / CO₂ / He fractions, initial and extinction diameter, burning rate, burn time and test end.
  • Cells that were unreadable characters in the CSV are treated as missing. One pressure listed as 0 mmHg (test 114) is treated as missing.
  • No values were filled in or estimated.

Data-quality findings (useful feedback for the repository)

  • BASS (PSI-26): the table is a test list with six columns and no test conditions or outcomes. Two dates are malformed ("5/15/2103", "6/29/`12").
  • BASS-II (PSI-25): no spread-rate, burn-time or outcome columns; outcomes exist only inside free-text flow logs. Five final-O₂ cells are implausible (0.6, 0.9, 1.6, 39.1, 40.4 %) and look like shifted columns. Tests T9 and T18 are dated 2018 among 2014 tests.
  • SAFFIRE-I (PSI-98): "Burn Time" is elapsed time, not a spread rate. SAFFIRE-II (PSI-99): the μ symbol is lost in the headers ("?-g"), and thickness "037 mm" appears to be 0.37 mm. SAFFIRE-III (PSI-100): configuration only, no results.
  • FLEX (PSI-69): about 30 diameter cells contain an unreadable character; the table does not say whether a cool flame followed the visible extinction.
  • SAME-R (PSI-101): the Rt/R0 column is not defined in the table. Values by material: .
  • ACME BRE (PSI-20): oxygen values such as "3400%" and "2100%" are formatting errors for 34 % and 21 %. CFI-G (PSI-159): the "Cool flame?" column is filled for only 8 of 179 normal-flame rows.
  • Not in PSI at all: partial-gravity combustion data (the challenge statement notes that LUCI and FM2 are expected to add it).

Not used

PSI-108 (STDCE-1) is a fluid-physics experiment on surface-tension flows and PSI-181 is a study of solder alloys. Both appeared in our first search and were excluded because they contain no combustion data.

How this simulator works

  • Outcome (data-fitted). A flame survives between a low-flow limit, below which it cannot get oxygen, and a blowoff limit, above which the flow is too fast for the chemistry. Both limits are exponential fits in oxygen level to the BASS-II extinction and blowoff points, separately for thin and thick fuels. No flame is predicted below 14 % O₂.
  • Gravity (model estimate). Gravity adds a buoyant flow to the ventilation: about 30 cm/s on Earth, scaled with g1/3 for the Moon (≈16 cm/s) and Mars (≈22 cm/s). PSI holds no partial-gravity combustion data, so these modes are estimates bounded by the microgravity and Earth cases. The model has no pressure term.
  • Field view (illustrative). Heat, oxygen deficit and smoke are carried by the airflow and by buoyancy and spread by diffusion on a 150 × 70 grid. This shows where heat and smoke go; it is not a CFD solution and the colours are relative, not temperatures.
  • Detectors. An alarm triggers when simulated smoke reaches the ceiling strip or the return duct. Times are in simulation seconds and are for comparing scenarios, not for predicting real alarm times.
  • Spread rates. Only the SAFFIRE-II values are measurements. Other values are scaled estimates and labelled as such.
  • Scope. An education and research-support tool, not a certified spacecraft safety prediction system.

How this simulator works

  • Prediction. For the chosen conditions the simulator finds the 7 most similar FLEX tests with the same fuel (similarity in O₂, CO₂, He, pressure and droplet size) and reports their distance-weighted outcomes. Nothing is predicted from theory.
  • Animation. The droplet shrinks following the classical d²-law, D² = D₀² − K·t, using the burning rate and burn time of the nearest tests. The flame drawing is schematic.
  • What "extinction" means. The visible flame went out with fuel left. A large extinction diameter and a short burn time mean the atmosphere suppressed the flame; a small one means the droplet burned almost completely first.
  • Limits. A single droplet is a model fire, not a burning material. Use it to compare suppressant gases and atmospheres, not to size an extinguisher.

Fire safety in space: what the data supports

NASA organises spacecraft fire safety around prevention, detection and suppression. Below, each measure is tied to the investigations that support it and to a simulator scenario you can run. Statements marked general practice come from published NASA material rather than from the tables used here.

1 · Prevention

Choose materials with microgravity data, not Earth tests alone

Materials are screened on Earth with an upward flame-spread test (NASA-STD-6001 Test 1). SAFFIRE-II shows the ranking can change in orbit: silicone burned in 1 g but did not spread in microgravity, Nomex did not ignite, and cotton-fiberglass fabric spread at 2.1–2.6 mm/s.

Evidence: SAFFIRE-II (PSI-99); BASS objective (PSI-26)

Treat oxygen level as the main design lever

BASS-II could not ignite a plastic rod at 13.6 % O₂ and found flames fragile near 15–16 %. Each extra percent of oxygen widens the range of airflow in which a fire survives. A 30–34 % habitat atmosphere sits far outside the tested range for solid materials.

Evidence: BASS-II (PSI-25) tests O4, O9, O12; ACME BRE (PSI-20) up to 40 % O₂

Do not rely on still air to keep materials safe at high oxygen

At 21 % O₂ a flame in still microgravity air starves. The model's low-flow limit falls as oxygen rises, and on the Moon or Mars buoyancy supplies airflow by itself. Materials for surface habitats need to be qualified for both.

Evidence: BASS-II low-flow limits; extrapolation above 22.2 % O₂; partial gravity is a model estimate

Control ignition sources and stowage

Electrical faults, overheated components and oxygen systems caused the historic incidents named in the challenge (Apollo 1, Mir). Keeping flammable items away from ignition sources and limiting how much burnable material is exposed remain the first line of defence.

general practice

2 · Detection

Put detectors where the air goes, not on the ceiling

Without buoyancy, smoke does not rise. It follows the ventilation flow. In the simulator the return-duct detector alarms in microgravity while the ceiling detector stays silent; on Earth it is the reverse. On the Moon and Mars both paths matter.

Evidence: field view (illustrative); SAME and SAME-R smoke transport objectives (PSI-102, PSI-101)

Detect first, then stop the airflow

Shutting off ventilation starves a microgravity fire, but it also stops smoke reaching duct-mounted detectors. Run the ventilation shut-off in the simulator before and after the alarm to see the trade-off.

Evidence: BASS-II low-flow extinction; simulator detector logic (illustrative)

Design for the smoke that forms in microgravity

SAME and SAME-R overheated Kapton, Teflon, silicone, lampwick and Pyrell on the ISS to measure the particles each releases. Detector sensitivity has to match those particle sizes, and discriminate smoke from dust (DAFT), which will matter with lunar regolith.

Evidence: SAME-R (PSI-101, 109 tests), SAME (PSI-102), DAFT (PSI-47)

A flame that looks out may not be out

FLEX and the Cool Flames Investigation found droplets that keep reacting at low temperature after the visible flame disappears. Fire-out criteria should not rely on visible flame alone.

Evidence: CFI (PSI-39), FLEX publications, CFI-G (PSI-159)

3 · Suppression and response

Ventilation shut-off works in orbit, at today's oxygen level

It is the one suppression action directly supported by solid-fuel data: 15 curated BASS-II flames went out as flow fell to about 1 cm/s or less. The same action is not expected to work in partial gravity, where buoyancy keeps supplying air.

Evidence: BASS-II (PSI-25); partial gravity is a model estimate

Diluting the atmosphere suppresses flames; the gas matters

FLEX measured droplet flames in atmospheres diluted with CO₂ and helium. In the droplet simulator, raise the suppressant fraction until the flame goes out quickly, then compare the two gases and the two pressures.

Evidence: FLEX (PSI-69), 274 tests
Open the droplet simulator

Lowering oxygen below about 14 % ends flaming of the tested plastic

The simulator's nitrogen-dilution response uses the BASS-II no-ignition result. In a crewed cabin this is constrained by what the crew can breathe, so it applies to isolated modules or uncrewed volumes.

Evidence: BASS-II test O12; general practice for crew limits

Portable extinguishers and crew procedure

Station crews are trained to protect breathing, remove power, stop airflow and use a portable extinguisher. The challenge statement notes the fine-water-mist extinguisher developed for the ISS. Extinguisher performance is not in the tables used here.

general practice

What this work adds for NASA

One view across separate investigations

PSI stores each investigation separately. FLARE-SI puts 20 of them on common axes (fuel, oxygen, pressure, airflow, measurements, fire-safety pillar) and ranks them for a stated need, which is the task set by the challenge.

A coverage and gap map for exploration atmospheres

The dashboard shows where data stops: no solid-fuel table above 22.2 % O₂, only 17 droplet tests at Moon-base-like atmospheres, and no partial-gravity data. This supports the case for experiments such as LUCI and FM2 and shows which existing data can bound them.

Model-ready extracts with provenance

41 curated solid-fuel outcomes and all 274 FLEX tests are provided as structured data with test IDs. The simulator shows they are enough to build and check simple predictive models, and labels every extrapolation.

Concrete repository feedback

The Data page lists specific problems found in the tables: missing condition columns, undefined variables, shifted cells, encoding errors. Fixing them would make the repository easier to use for everyone.

A training and outreach tool

The simulator makes counter-intuitive results visible in seconds: a flame that dies when the fan stops, a detector on the ceiling that never alarms. It is suited to students, new engineers and crew familiarisation.

Relevance on Earth

The same data supports terrestrial fire science: smoke-versus-dust discrimination for detectors, low-oxygen fire prevention in enclosed spaces, and suppressant-gas comparisons.

About, methods and disclosures

Use of artificial intelligence

  • AI tool used during development: Claude (Anthropic), a large language model.
  • Code: the website, both simulators, the ranking rule and the evidence assistant were generated with the AI tool to the team's requirements. Each script carries a comment saying so.
  • Data: the AI tool retrieved the PSI tables, extracted the 41 solid-fuel outcomes from BASS-II free-text logs and reviewed table quality. NASA's values were not altered. Each extracted row keeps its PSI test ID so anyone can check it against the source table.
  • Text: investigation summaries, the "what it means" interpretations and the project report were drafted with the AI tool from PSI tables and metadata.
  • Not AI-generated: all experimental values (NASA PSI). No AI-generated images, video or audio. No NASA logos or mission insignia.
  • At run time: no AI model is called. The evidence assistant is a rule-based search over the loaded tables, so it cannot invent values. Rankings come from the scoring rule shown here, and the interpretations on the dashboard are fixed text, kept separate from the assistant's answers.

Ranking rule

Each investigation has a 0–3 relevance to prevention, detection and suppression, and 0–3 feature levels: elevated oxygen, reduced pressure, solid fuels, large scale, airflow varied, partial-gravity relevance, and per-test quantitative outcomes in the table.

score = 12 × pillar relevance (or 5 × each pillar for "All") + Σ habitat weight × feature + (6 or 2) × quantitative + 2 × log₁₀(rows + 1)

Relevance and feature levels were assigned by the team from each table and its metadata. They are judgements, shown openly on each investigation's detail card.

Limitations

  • Only experimental tables were analysed, not raw video or sensor data.
  • The evidence assistant understands keywords, not free language. It returns per-test rows for BASS-II, SAFFIRE and FLEX, and table descriptions for the other investigations.
  • The solid-fuel limits are fitted to 41 outcomes and checked against the same tests; there is no independent validation set yet.
  • Partial-gravity and Earth modes are model estimates. The solid-fuel model has no pressure dependence.
  • The field view is illustrative, not a CFD solution.
  • This is not a certified safety tool and must not be used for flight decisions.
